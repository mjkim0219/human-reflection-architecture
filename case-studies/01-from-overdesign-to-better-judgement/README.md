# Case Study 01

# From Overdesign to Better Judgment

## Context

When I first started designing software architecture, I believed that good implementation required a nearly perfect design.

Because I had little experience in software engineering and AI, I tried to eliminate uncertainty before writing any code.

As a result, implementation rarely began.

---

## Initial Judgment

> Better design comes before implementation.

I believed every important decision should be made before building anything.

---

## What Changed?

There was no single turning point.

Instead, my judgment gradually changed through repeated exposure to AI-assisted development, architecture discussions, mentors, implementation experience, and continuous reflection.

Eventually I realized something simple.

> Implementation is not the reward for certainty.

It is one of the ways certainty is built.

---

## Reflection

The problem was not that my design was incomplete.

The problem was believing that uncertainty had to disappear before implementation.

Instead of waiting for certainty, I began treating implementation itself as an experiment that could produce evidence.

---

## Evidence

Several observable changes followed.

- I declared a design freeze for v1.0.
- I reduced unnecessary architectural responsibilities.
- I started implementing before expanding the design.
- I introduced an Owner Review process instead of trying to solve everything myself.
- I published the repository before considering it "finished."

These were not changes in tools.

They were changes in judgment.

---

## Updated Judgment

Instead of pursuing perfect design first, I adopted a different principle.

> Design enough to build.

> Build enough to learn.

> Learn before expanding.

---

## Outcome

The architecture stopped expanding indefinitely.

Implementation finally became possible.

More importantly, I developed my own criteria for judgment instead of relying on the illusion of perfect planning.

---

## Limitations

This case does not prove that implementation-first is always the correct approach.

It documents one instance where structured reflection changed a long-held design assumption.

Future case studies may support, refine, or even challenge this conclusion.

---

## Author's Note

For a long time, I believed better design meant eliminating uncertainty before implementation.

Now I think differently.

You don't need a perfect design.

You need a design that is good enough to learn.
